# Three launch options

September 20, 2026. These are product hypotheses derived from the linked evidence in [the report](RESEARCH-REPORT.md), not ready-to-manufacture specifications. Prices, costs and validation thresholds below are our proposed assumptions. No demand experiment has yet been run.

## 1. Unflavored daily fiber — first test

**Customer:** adults who want a consistent fiber routine but dislike texture, sweetness or an unnecessarily complex blend. Do not define the initial audience by a disease or medication.

**Hero product:** one unflavored PHGG-based powder in a 30-serving tub or refill. Final ingredient, dose, directions, compatibility and claims need qualified formulation review. Compare a single-fiber formula with existing products before adding probiotics, collagen or electrolytes. More ingredients would add cost and make it harder to understand tolerance.

**Offer hypothesis:** $29 every 30 days, optional one-time purchase at $34. Allow 30/45/60-day scheduling, show the next charge and send reminders. Start with the simplest pack; travel sachets can wait until repeat demand supports the packaging cost.

**Why someone would switch:** better mixing and a routine they prefer at an acceptable price. “Gentle,” “no bloat” and “clinically proven” are not automatic properties of the concept. The claim must match the actual evidence.

**Competitive hurdle:** Hilma is cheaper; Regular Girl already uses PHGG; Momentous sells fiber with a strong established brand. A generic white-label powder with a new label is insufficient differentiation. See the price table and links in the report.

**First creative tests:** demonstrate actual mixing in water and a familiar food; explain the label and serving size; present the full renewal offer. Use measured product facts, not simulated clinical outcomes or medication-side-effect promises.

**Validation gate:** interview 15 recent fiber buyers, including five who stopped using one. After appropriate product and participant review, compare prototypes with two incumbents in a small blinded preference test. Proposed directional gate: at least 18 of 30 prefer the prototype's use experience and at least 10 are willing to purchase at the real $29 price. This is not statistical proof or a clinical trial. Stop for unresolved tolerance or quality concerns. Before expansion, observe actual day-30 consumption and day-60 paid reorders.

**Kill conditions:** no preference advantage, most demand exists only below sustainable pricing, or adverse tolerance makes the routine unreliable. Confidence: moderate as a test priority, unvalidated as a business.

## 2. Creatine for women returning to strength training — distribution-led alternative

**Customer:** women approximately 35–55 beginning or resuming resistance training who want clear, credible guidance. Age and gender define communication and community, not proof that they require a special creatine molecule.

**Hero product:** plain creatine monohydrate powder, 60 daily servings, with serving details set through formulation review. Publish appropriate batch testing and explain product use without promising hormone balance, menopause treatment, guaranteed mental clarity or results without training.

**Offer hypothesis:** $36 per 60-day shipment; $40 optional one-time purchase. That is $18 per 30 days for a continuing subscriber. Guidance, reminders and a simple training habit tracker are part of the service, but should not be presented as medical care.

**Why someone would switch:** trust in the coach/community, understandable instructions, predictable quality and a routine they follow. The powder alone is easy to substitute. Momentous's normalized continuing price makes a premium difficult to defend.

**First creative tests:** explain common use questions; show the plain powder and label; feature a genuine training routine. Avoid framing creatine as interchangeable with caffeine or claiming it cannot disturb anyone's sleep.

**Validation gate:** work first with a credible coach or existing training community. Interview 15 target users about current alternatives and the value of guidance. Compare the proposed offer with an established commodity product at transparent prices. Require real purchase intent at the actual price, then measure use and second orders at day 60. A large email list alone is not validation.

**Kill conditions:** buyers choose solely by price, guidance has little perceived value, or acquisition depends on unsupported health promises. Confidence: moderate-low; stronger if a distribution advantage already exists.

## 3. Light-tasting unsweetened hydration — conditional option

**Customer:** frequent recreational runners or gym users who dislike sweet drinks and want to keep hydration separate from carbohydrate fuel. Do not generalize medical-community sodium needs to every customer.

**Hero product:** one lightly flavored, unsweetened electrolyte powder; start with a resealable 30-serving package to test use before funding several stick-pack flavors. Sodium amount, directions and positioning require formulation review and a defined use case.

**Offer hypothesis:** $34 per 30-serving shipment, initially every 45 days, with adjustable scheduling and a $38 one-time option. Consumption may be seasonal or training-dependent. Do not force a daily-use subscription merely to increase apparent MRR.

**Why someone would switch:** demonstrably preferred taste, fewer ingredients they dislike, or a trusted local training channel. LMNT Raw and Skratch Unsweetened already exist. “Unsweetened” alone is not an uncovered market.

**First creative tests:** show taste preference results honestly with methods; explain hydration versus fuel; demonstrate the intended mixing ratio. Avoid claiming universal dehydration, treating POTS, preventing heat illness or replacing workplace heat controls.

**Validation gate:** blind-test against Skratch Unsweetened and LMNT Raw at intended dilution, then reveal price. Require both a preference advantage and willingness to pay; track actual consumption over a training cycle. Recruit people who dislike sweet mixes, not only enthusiastic friends.

**Kill conditions:** taste is merely equivalent, refill use is too infrequent, or shipping costs erase the perceived price advantage. Confidence: low-moderate; reserve until a specific advantage is demonstrated.

## Shared sequence and economic checks

1. Confirm the use case, current alternatives and real spending through interviews. No outreach has been sent as part of this research.
2. Obtain comparable supplier quotes for formula, pack, minimum order, testing, lead time and landed cost. Obtain identity, potency, contaminant and stability documentation appropriate to the chosen product. There is no approved manufacturer in this package.
3. Resolve label and advertising claims for the actual formula. Ingredient research does not automatically substantiate the finished product or every audience claim.
4. Run the smallest useful preference and willingness-to-pay test; use predeclared criteria and record negative results.
5. Pilot one SKU. Measure paid second-order conversion by cohort, consumption, refunds, complaints and contribution after acquisition.

The [economics CSV](../exports/unit-economics.csv) and dashboard are scenarios. The 90-day example assumes 65% of the original cohort places a second order and 45% places a third where cadence permits. It does not assume 65% retention repeatedly. Orders scheduled on day 90 are excluded from the interval [0,90). Discounting, failed payments, returns and fulfillment changes should be modeled from actual pilot data before scaling.

**Preferred choice:** fiber if product preference can be established; creatine if you already have trusted access to the audience; hydration only after a clear head-to-head win. A failed validation should lead to stopping or changing the offer, not adding more ingredients to justify it.
